Historical Introduction to the Cognitive Science

- Artur Galvão e José António Alves   (Translation: Alfredo Dinis)

 

Cognitive Sciences have a very long past but a rather short history. They go back to the debates among the Greek philosophers on the nature of the human mind, and are today identified mainly with Artificial Intelligence, Neurosciences, Philosophy, Cognitive Linguistics and Cognitive Psychology.

Attempts at a full understanding of the mind are not specific to western thought. However in eastern cultures, interest for the human mind has been taking place within the context of transcendental, religious and cosmic speculations, and for this reason it is difficult to come to a scientific understanding of the results attained so far. Nevertheless, nowadays some authors in the area of the cognitive sciences have become interested in the study of the budist tradition.

In western countries not only the Greek but also the medieval authors have given a valid contribution to the study of what we name today cognitive sciences, since they tried to understand what is mind, its structure, origin, relation with the body and possible immortality. We all love reading truly immortal works such as Plato’s Phaedrus, Aristotle’s De anima, Augustine’s Confessions, Thomas Aquinas’ Summa Theologica, etc.

However, the traditional image of the human being we have cherished until today acquired its final traits during the seventeenth and the eighteenth centuries under the influence of a stream of thought that includes Descartes, Locke and Kant.

Throughout his philosophical works, Descartes, who some consider the father of modernity, aimed especially at establishing a method of attaining a knowledge that might be so valid and certain that not even the most stubborn sceptics would challenge it.

He used the method of introspection and conceived himself as composed of mind and body. He considered the mind as pure thought, free from the influence of both physical and biological factors. He thought of the body as a material and divisible entity, subject to physical causality and death. From this point of view, man’s bodily existence was that of an automat, something similar to a biological machine. It is the mind that is the source of rational principles, of dignity and of immortality, and it thus represents the specific dimension that constitutes human beings as such.

Against this introspectionist view, the behaviourist paradigm arose, both in psychology and in philosophy, at the beginning of the twentieth century. It aimed at explaining every aspect of human activity without any reference to inner mental processes, following a methodology of public observation of people’s behaviour. Individuals were seen as passive reflectors of the several forces and factors originating in the environment where they lived, and their behaviour was seen as quantifiable.

However, since both the methods and the results of behaviourism appeared unsatisfactory, new interdisciplinary studies aimed at creating a new science of the mind– cybernetics – were developed in the forties and the fifties. Studies carried out by some authors, such as Turing and von Newman in logic and mathematics, Lasheley in psychology, McCulloch and Pitts in neurophysiology, and Shannon and Wiener in information theory, have created the adequate context for the development of cybernetics and led to its subsequent replacement by the cognitive sciences. A double aspect of the cybernetic movement needs to be underlined. Firstly, it challenged and replaced the behaviourist paradigm, whose simplistic theoretical framework had been uncovered. Secondly, it proposed the computational metaphor as the central image of the human mind. Such a metaphor appeared to be so adequate to the study of mind that within just a few decades both psychologists and neuroscientists would not say that the brain was like a computer but rather that it was a computer. The top of a long list of brain metaphors was thus reached. In different times and according to the dominant ideas in every one of them, such metaphors aimed at representing the mind as wax plaques, a weaver’s loom, valves, watches, tubes, machines, hydraulic systems, telegraphs and telephonic communications!

In 1956 a six-week meeting took place in the United States at Darmouth. The best world experts in computing science gathered there aiming at understanding mental processes on the basis of the computational model. This model allowed them to leave behaviourism behind without falling into the vacuity of introspectionism. The relevance of this meeting is such that 1956 is now taken as the official date of the birth of the cognitive sciences.

The impact of the computational metaphor is due both to the clarity and the simplicity of its explanation of mind, and to its ability to simulate the different human mental processes. It is therefore no surprise that cognitive sciences have grown mainly out of Artificial Intelligence, and that this fact is at the origin of the cognitivist movement.

From the eighties onwards, conexionism has been developed as an alternative approach to cognitivism. This new model takes up McCulloch and Pitts research on neural networks. Against cognitivism, conexionism takes a ‘bottom up’ approach, moving from the simplest elements of the brain (neurons), aiming at understanding human mental abilities of higher levels, such as thought, problem resolution, etc.

While cognitivism and conexionism continue to guide distinct research programs, new explanatory models have been proposed, such as enactivism which has been put forward by Varela and Maturana of the Chile School.

It was long ago that the first approaches to the mind-body problem were developed, but many open questions still remain. Although we have made some progress until now, the future is still full of surprises. We have a long way to go not only along the roads that are already open, but also along the new ways that will surely be discovered.
